# The Hypothesis Testing Framework: Validating Business Decisions

## Introduction
In business operations, gut feelings are not enough to drive strategy. When stakeholders ask, "Did the new website design actually increase sales, or was it just a lucky week?", you need a mathematical framework to answer them. 

Hypothesis testing is the engine of data-driven decision-making. It transitions an analyst from simply observing a trend to statistically proving whether a specific business intervention had a real, measurable impact.

---

## 1. The Core Terminology: The Two Hypotheses
Every statistical test begins by framing the business problem into two opposing statements.

### The Null Hypothesis ($H_0$)
The Null Hypothesis represents the **status quo**. It assumes that nothing has changed, there is no effect, or there is no difference between groups. 
* **Business Example:** The new checkout layout has *no effect* on the conversion rate compared to the old layout.

### The Alternative Hypothesis ($H_1$ or $H_a$)
The Alternative Hypothesis represents your **discovery**. It is the claim you are trying to prove—that an intervention, change, or relationship actually exists.
* **Business Example:** The new checkout layout *increases* the conversion rate compared to the old layout.

*Goal: To gather enough data to confidently reject the Null Hypothesis in favour of the Alternative.*

---

## 2. The P-Value and Statistical Significance ($\alpha$)

To decide which hypothesis is true, we rely on two critical metrics:

### Significance Level ($\alpha$)
Before running any test, a business must define its tolerance for risk. This is the significance level ($\alpha$, pronounced "alpha"). 
* **Standard Practice:** $\alpha = 0.05$ (A 5% threshold).
* This means you demand a 95% confidence level before you will advise the business to make a change.

### The P-Value
The p-value is the probability of observing your data *if the Null Hypothesis were entirely true*. 
* **The Golden Rule:** * If **p-value $\le \alpha$**: The result is statistically significant. **Reject $H_0$.** (The change worked).
  * If **p-value > $\alpha$**: Not enough evidence. **Fail to reject $H_0$.** (Keep the status quo).

---

## 3. The Cost of Being Wrong: Type I and Type II Errors

No statistical test is 100% perfect. In the real world, errors cost money. You must learn to balance two types of risks:

* **Type I Error (False Positive):** You reject $H_0$ when it is actually true.
  * *Business Impact:* You tell leadership the new marketing campaign works. They invest millions. It actually does nothing. (This rate is controlled by your $\alpha$).
* **Type II Error (False Negative):** You fail to reject $H_0$ when $H_1$ is actually true.
  * *Business Impact:* You conclude a highly profitable algorithm doesn't work because your sample size was too small. You abandon a great idea and lose potential revenue.

---

## 4. Choosing the Right Test: Z-Test vs. T-Test

When comparing means (averages) between groups (e.g., Average Order Value of Group A vs Group B), you will typically choose between a Z-Test and a T-Test.

### The Z-Test
Used when you have a **large sample size ($n > 30$)** AND you know the population standard deviation (which is rare in reality). Often used in large-scale e-commerce datasets where data points number in the tens of thousands.

### The T-Test
Used when dealing with **smaller sample sizes ($n \le 30$)** or when the population standard deviation is unknown. 
* **One-Sample T-Test:** Compares your sample mean to a known target (e.g., "Are our average deliveries taking longer than the promised 3 days?").
* **Two-Sample (Independent) T-Test:** Compares two separate groups (e.g., "Is the average spend higher for customers in Indore compared to Bengaluru?").

---

## 5. Implementation in Python

Here is how to perform an Independent Two-Sample T-Test using `scipy.stats` to analyse whether a new logistics partner delivers faster than the current one.

``` python
import pandas as pd
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

# Simulated delivery times in days for two different logistics vendors
vendor_a_times = np.array([4.2, 3.8, 4.1, 5.0, 3.9, 4.5, 4.0, 3.7])
vendor_b_times = np.array([3.1, 3.3, 3.0, 3.6, 3.2, 3.1, 2.9, 3.4])

# Define our Alpha
alpha = 0.05

# Perform Independent Two-Sample T-Test
t_stat, p_value = stats.ttest_ind(vendor_a_times, vendor_b_times)

print(f"T-Statistic: {t_stat:.4f}")
print(f"P-Value: {p_value:.6f}")

if p_value <= alpha:
    print("Conclusion: Reject the Null Hypothesis. There is a significant difference in delivery times.")
else:
    print("Conclusion: Fail to reject the Null Hypothesis. Not enough evidence to prove a difference.")
```
