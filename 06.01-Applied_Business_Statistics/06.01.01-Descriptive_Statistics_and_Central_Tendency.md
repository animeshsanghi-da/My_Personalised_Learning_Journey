# Descriptive Statistics and Central Tendency

## Introduction
Before building complex predictive models or designing executive dashboards, you must understand the shape, spread, and behaviour of your raw data. Descriptive statistics serve as the foundation of data analysis. They allow you to summarise large datasets—such as millions of transaction records—into a few easily digestible metrics. 

When analysing business data, stakeholders rarely want to see every row; they want the bottom line. Descriptive statistics provide that bottom line, helping you identify trends, spot anomalies, and establish a baseline before applying advanced machine learning algorithms.

---

## 1. Measures of Central Tendency
Central tendency tells us where the "middle" or "typical" value of a dataset lies. Depending on the distribution of your data, different measures provide different business insights.

### The Mean (Average)
* **What it is:** The sum of all values divided by the number of values.
* **When to use it:** When your data is symmetrically distributed without extreme outliers.
* **Business Context:** Calculating the average order value (AOV) for an e-commerce platform. However, if one customer buys a high-end server while others buy phone cases, the mean will be heavily skewed and misleading.

### The Median (The Middle Value)
* **What it is:** The exact middle value when the data is sorted from smallest to largest. If there is an even number of observations, it is the average of the two middle numbers.
* **When to use it:** When your data has outliers or is skewed. The median is heavily resistant to outliers.
* **Business Context:** Analysing delivery times in the Olist dataset. If 99% of packages arrive in 3 days, but a few get lost and take 60 days, the median accurately reflects the typical customer experience, whereas the mean would artificially inflate the expected delivery time.

### The Mode (The Most Frequent)
* **What it is:** The value that appears most often in a dataset.
* **When to use it:** Primarily for categorical data or discrete numbers.
* **Business Context:** Identifying the most popular payment method (e.g., Credit Card vs. UPI) or the most frequently purchased product category.

---

## 2. Measures of Dispersion (Spread)
Knowing the average is never enough. You must also know how spread out the data is. High dispersion means high volatility, which often translates to higher business risk.

### Range
* **What it is:** The difference between the maximum and minimum values.
* **Limitation:** Highly sensitive to outliers. It only considers the two extreme points.

### Variance and Standard Deviation
* **Variance:** The average of the squared differences from the Mean. (Squaring ensures negative and positive distances don't cancel each other out).
* **Standard Deviation (σ):** The square root of the variance. This is the most critical metric because it brings the unit of measurement back to the original unit of the data.
* **Business Context:** If a manufacturing process for USB bulbs produces items with a high standard deviation in lifespan, quality control is failing. A low standard deviation means the process is consistent and predictable.

### Interquartile Range (IQR)
* **What it is:** The difference between the 75th percentile (Q3) and the 25th percentile (Q1). It represents the middle 50% of your data.
* **Business Context:** Excellent for understanding the core behaviour of your customers while ignoring the absolute highest and lowest spenders.

---

## 3. Practical Application: Identifying Outliers
Outliers can destroy the accuracy of statistical models like Linear Regression. You must identify and handle them during the ETL and data processing phases. 

The standard statistical method for finding outliers relies on the IQR:
1. Calculate **Q1** (25th percentile) and **Q3** (75th percentile).
2. Calculate **IQR** = Q3 - Q1.
3. Define the **Lower Bound** = Q1 - (1.5 * IQR).
4. Define the **Upper Bound** = Q3 + (1.5 * IQR).
*Any data point falling outside these bounds is statistically considered an outlier.*

---

## 4. Implementation in Python (Pandas)
In modern analytics workflows, Pandas handles these calculations instantly. 

``` python
import pandas as pd
import numpy as np

# Load sample e-commerce data
# Assuming 'df' is a previously loaded DataFrame containing 'order_value'
df = pd.DataFrame({'order_value': [15, 22, 18, 25, 30, 22, 1050, 19, 21, 28]})

# 1. Central Tendency
mean_val = df['order_value'].mean()
median_val = df['order_value'].median()
mode_val = df['order_value'].mode()[0]

print(f"Mean: {mean_val}, Median: {median_val}, Mode: {mode_val}")
# Notice how the single $1050 order heavily skews the mean, but the median remains stable.

# 2. Dispersion
std_dev = df['order_value'].std()
print(f"Standard Deviation: {std_dev}")

# 3. Outlier Detection using IQR
Q1 = df['order_value'].quantile(0.25)
Q3 = df['order_value'].quantile(0.75)
IQR = Q3 - Q1

lower_bound = Q1 - 1.5 * IQR
upper_bound = Q3 + 1.5 * IQR

# Filtering the dataset to remove outliers
clean_df = df[(df['order_value'] >= lower_bound) & (df['order_value'] <= upper_bound)]

print(f"Original shape: {df.shape}")
print(f"Cleaned shape: {clean_df.shape}")
```

## 5. Strategic Takeaway
When presenting findings to stakeholders, avoid throwing raw numbers at them. Instead of saying *"The standard deviation is high,"* translate the statistics into a business narrative: *"Our delivery times are highly inconsistent, meaning we cannot reliably promise 3-day shipping to our customers. We need to investigate the logistical bottlenecks causing these outliers."* Mastering descriptive statistics ensures that the foundational layer of your data visualisations and machine learning models is mathematically sound.
