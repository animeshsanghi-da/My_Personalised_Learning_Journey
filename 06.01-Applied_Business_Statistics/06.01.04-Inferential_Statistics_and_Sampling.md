# Inferential Statistics & Sampling: From Data to Decisions

## Introduction
Descriptive statistics help you summarise and understand the data you currently hold. However, in enterprise analytics, you rarely have access to data for every single customer, transaction, or event in existence. 

Inferential statistics bridge this gap. They allow you to take a smaller, manageable dataset (a **sample**) and make mathematically sound predictions or draw conclusions about the larger group (the **population**). Whether you are estimating the average delivery time for all future Brazilian e-commerce orders or predicting regional sales trends, inferential statistics provide the mathematical proof that your business recommendations are reliable.

---

## 1. The Core Concept: Population vs. Sample
* **Population (N):** The complete set of all items or individuals you are interested in. (e.g., Every single customer who has ever purchased from a business).
* **Sample (n):** A subset of the population used for analysis. (e.g., 5,000 randomly selected customers).

**The Business Problem:** Calculating the exact mean (μ) or standard deviation (σ) of a massive population is often computationally expensive, time-consuming, or impossible. Instead, we calculate the sample mean (x̄) and sample standard deviation (s) to *estimate* the population parameters.

---

## 2. Sampling Techniques
To ensure your sample accurately reflects the population, you must select your data carefully. Poor sampling leads to biased data and catastrophic business decisions.

### A. Probability Sampling (Best for Analytics)
1.  **Simple Random Sampling:** Every row in your database has an equal chance of being selected.
    * *Use Case:* Generating a general baseline metric when the population is relatively uniform.
2.  **Stratified Sampling:** The population is divided into subgroups (strata) based on a shared characteristic (e.g., geographical regions like Indore, Bengaluru, Pune), and random samples are drawn proportionally from each.
    * *Use Case:* Ensuring minority segments or smaller regions are adequately represented in your final dataset.
3.  **Systematic Sampling:** Selecting every *k*-th record from a list (e.g., every 10th transaction).
    * *Use Case:* Quick pipeline quality checks during data ingestion.
4.  **Cluster Sampling:** Dividing the population into random clusters (e.g., specific store branches) and fully analysing a few randomly chosen clusters.

### B. Non-Probability Sampling (Avoid in Rigorous Analysis)
* **Convenience Sampling:** Picking data that is easiest to access. Highly prone to bias and should not be used to drive strategic business models.

---

## 3. The Central Limit Theorem (CLT)
The Central Limit Theorem is arguably the most important concept in applied statistics. 

**The Theorem:** Regardless of the underlying distribution of the population (whether it is normal, skewed, or uniform), the distribution of the *sample means* will approach a normal distribution as the sample size gets larger. 

* **The Magic Number:** In practice, a sample size of **n ≥ 30** is generally considered sufficient for the CLT to hold true.
* **Why it matters:** Because the sample means become normally distributed, we can use standard normal probability formulas (Z-scores, T-tests) to analyse practically any business metric, even if the raw data is heavily skewed.

---

## 4. Confidence Intervals and Margin of Error
When you present a finding to stakeholders, giving a single number (a "point estimate") is risky. If you say, "The average customer lifetime value is $150," you are almost certainly slightly wrong. 

Instead, analysts use **Confidence Intervals (CI)** to provide a range.

* **Confidence Interval:** A range of values that is likely to contain the true population parameter, accompanied by a level of confidence (usually 90%, 95%, or 99%).
    * *Example:* "We are 95% confident that the true average customer lifetime value is between $142 and $158."
* **Margin of Error:** The radius of the confidence interval. In the example above, the margin of error is $8.

**The Trade-off:** To increase your confidence level (e.g., moving from 95% to 99%), your interval must become wider, making the insight slightly less precise. To maintain a narrow interval with high confidence, you must increase your sample size.

## 5. Implementation in Python

When working in environments like VS Code or Jupyter Lab, you can easily compute confidence intervals using `scipy` and `numpy`.

``` python
import numpy as np
import scipy.stats as st
import pandas as pd

# Simulating an array of delivery times (in days) from a sample of 150 orders
np.random.seed(42)
sample_delivery_times = np.random.normal(loc=12.5, scale=3.2, size=150)

# Calculate sample mean and standard error
sample_mean = np.mean(sample_delivery_times)
standard_error = st.sem(sample_delivery_times)

# Calculate the 95% Confidence Interval using a T-distribution
confidence_level = 0.95
degrees_freedom = len(sample_delivery_times) - 1

confidence_interval = st.t.interval(
    confidence=confidence_level,
    df=degrees_freedom,
    loc=sample_mean,
    scale=standard_error
)

print(f"Sample Mean: {sample_mean:.2f} days")
print(f"95% Confidence Interval: {confidence_interval[0]:.2f} to {confidence_interval[1]:.2f} days")

# Business Translation:
# "Based on our sample, we are 95% confident that the true average delivery 
# time for the entire population of orders is between the lower and upper bounds."
```
